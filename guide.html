1 · Smoother games

What actually makes games smoother, most impact first.

Stutter is a frame-time problem, not an FPS problem: a game can average 200 FPS and still hitch. These are the fixes that testing backs up, in the order worth trying them. Pulse marks the ones Pulse checks or does for you.

1

Make sure your monitor runs at its full refresh rate, from the graphics card Pulse

A 144 Hz screen left at 60 Hz, or a cable plugged into the motherboard instead of the graphics card, costs more than any tweak can win back. Check Windows Settings → System → Display → Advanced display, and plug the cable into the ports on the graphics card itself.

2

Turn on XMP or EXPO in the BIOS Pulse

Without it, RAM runs at its slow fallback speed: DDR4 at 3,200 MHz and DDR5 at 4,800 MHz. It's a one-time BIOS switch and one of the biggest free gains, especially in CPU-heavy games. It does count as overclocking, so stability varies, especially with four sticks.

3

Enable Resizable BAR (Smart Access Memory on AMD)

It lets the CPU see all of the graphics card's memory at once. Gains depend on the game: typically a few percent, and over 15% in some titles. In the BIOS: boot in UEFI mode with CSM off, turn on Above 4G Decoding, then Re-Size BAR Support. NVIDIA's Control Panel → System Information shows whether it's on.

4

Let shaders build, and stop wiping them

Modern games compile shaders the first time an effect appears, which causes hitches that fade on a second run. Let the game finish its pre-compile step, keep Steam's shader pre-caching on, and raise the driver's shader cache size (NVIDIA: 10 GB or Unlimited). Avoid "cleaner" apps that delete the cache: they bring the stutter back. Only clear it if a game misbehaves after a driver update.

5

Use G-SYNC or FreeSync with the right frame cap Pulse

Turn on variable refresh in the monitor and the driver, keep V-Sync on in the driver and off in the game. With NVIDIA Reflex or Ultra Low Latency on, the driver caps for you (about 138 FPS at 144 Hz, 224 at 240 Hz). Without Reflex, cap a few FPS below your refresh rate, ideally with the game's own limiter.

6

Power mode, Game Mode and background recording Pulse

The settings that still measurably help in Windows 11: power mode on Best performance (laptops: when plugged in), Game Mode on, variable refresh and windowed-game optimizations on, Xbox Game Bar's background recording off, and fewer startup apps.

7

Keep games on an SSD, and watch the temperatures Pulse

Several new games stutter badly on hard drives. Drops that start after 5 to 15 minutes usually mean heat: clean dust, fix airflow, repaste or undervolt. Pulse's load test shows whether your CPU throttles.

8

Memory Integrity: test before you turn it off

Windows started switching Memory Integrity (a kernel security feature) on by default for eligible PCs with the October 13, 2026 update. Old tests measured up to 15% in the worst cases, but 2026 tests on modern CPUs found under 2% (CS2: 398 vs 405 FPS). Our take: leave it on unless you have an older CPU and measure a real difference, because turning it off removes real protection.

2 · Tweaks to skip

Popular "FPS boosts" that don't help.

Registry cleaners

No measurable effect on any modern Windows, and a real chance of breaking installed software.

Turning off the page file

Games and Windows itself expect one. Removing it causes crashes when memory runs out, not more speed.

Old registry "FPS packs"

Many 2017-era tweaks no longer apply in Windows 11 24H2 and later. Pulse leaves out tweaks Microsoft documents as unused.

3 · Hardware news

What's happening with PC hardware (November 2026).

One story is behind most of it: AI data centers are buying most of the world's memory, so RAM, SSDs and graphics cards all cost far more than a year ago.

RAM

32 GB of DDR5 now costs $430 to $600

The cheapest 2×16 GB DDR5-6000 CL30 kit went from $59 in April 2025 to $429 on November 3, 2026. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron moved production to AI servers, and Micron closed its Crucial consumer brand in December 2025. DDR4 is up sharply too.

RAM

The worst may still be ahead

On November 2, Team Group's CEO told investors the tightest stretch runs from Q4 2026 to Q2 2027, with supply and demand balancing in the second half of 2028 or later.

Storage

SSDs are climbing as well

Some SSD contract prices are up 303% in a year, and TrendForce expects another 15 to 20% rise this quarter.

GPU

Graphics cards far above launch price

NVIDIA's own store pulled the RTX 5090 and then the RTX 5080 as street prices hit over twice MSRP. AMD is reportedly planning its fourth Radeon price rise this year (not confirmed by AMD).

GPU

AMD outsold NVIDIA on Amazon US

For the first time, according to sales tracker TechEpiphany on November 3, Radeon cards outsold GeForce in units on Amazon US, as GeForce prices climbed. That's one retailer, not the whole market.

Coming

Next-gen graphics cards: likely 2028

Leakers report NVIDIA's RTX 60 series and most of AMD's RDNA 5 cards won't arrive until 2028, with one AMD chip possibly in 2027. Neither company has confirmed a date.

Coming

New CPUs: early 2027

AMD's Zen 6 desktop chips and Intel's Nova Lake are expected around CES 2027, not this year. Zen 6 stays on AM5, so today's AM5 boards should take it.

Software

Windows 11 26H2 is out

Released September 29 as a small enablement package: few visible changes, and it resets support to 24 months on Home and Pro. NVIDIA's DLSS 5 is also rolling into more games.

4 · Upgrading now

How to upgrade without paying shortage prices.

The usual "add more RAM" advice is expensive right now. Here's what gets you the most for the money.

  • Get the free speed first. XMP/EXPO, Resizable BAR and the right monitor settings cost nothing.
  • Buy RAM as a matched pair. DDR4 and DDR5 don't mix, and adding two more sticks can make a PC slower. A 2×16 GB kit is the sensible size for gaming.
  • Used RAM and graphics cards, new SSDs. Used memory and GPUs can still sell below new prices; SSDs wear out, so buy those new.
  • Upgrade what isn't in short supply. CPUs, monitors, mice and keyboards haven't been hit as hard, and a faster monitor changes how every game feels.
  • Lean on upscaling. DLSS, FSR and XeSS let a mid-range card run higher resolutions smoothly.
